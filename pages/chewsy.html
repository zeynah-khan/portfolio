University project · Mobile app

Chewsy — helping students stop wasting food, and money

A smart meal planner that starts from what's already in your fridge, not a shopping list — built around a real, well-researched problem rather than a nice-to-have feature.

Role
UX research, IA, wireframing, UI design, prototyping
Timeline
University coursework, extended independently
Tools
Figma, secondary research, usability testing
The problem

UK households waste around 6 million tonnes of food a year, and roughly three-quarters of that was edible (WRAP, 2022). Students are a big part of this — unpredictable schedules, limited cooking confidence, and poor visibility into what's already in the fridge all combine to make waste the default outcome.

Secondary research (Food Standards Agency, Zero Waste Scotland) put real numbers behind it: around two-thirds of students reported food in their fridge past its use-by date, and the average student loses roughly £273 a year to avoidable waste.

Existing apps like Mealime and Tasty solve a different problem — they assume you're shopping for a recipe, not cooking from what you've already got. Neither tracks expiry dates or budgets for students specifically. That gap became Chewsy's reason to exist.

Who I designed for

I built a persona from patterns across the research and early empathy mapping, to keep decisions anchored to a real kind of user rather than an abstract "student."

Zara, 20 — second-year student in shared accommodation with limited kitchen space. Juggling coursework, a part-time job, and a full social life. Grocery shops are quick and unplanned. Goals: stop throwing money away on unused food, spend less mental energy figuring out what to cook. Her key frustration, captured in the empathy map: "I have lots of ingredients but I'm not sure what to make with them."

The interface

Home, ingredient input, pantry tracker, and recipe detail — the core flow, taken to full visual design.

Home, "What Can I Cook?", Pantry Tracker, and Recipe Detail.
Design decisions

The interface is built around a fridge-as-inventory mental model — the pantry tracker mirrors how students already think about their fridge, rather than introducing an unfamiliar system.

  • Colour-coded urgency (fresh / soon / use now) turns "check your fridge" from a task into something scannable at a glance.
  • Ingredient-first search, not recipe-first — the app starts from what you have, directly answering the most common frustration in research.
  • "In your pantry" tags on recipe ingredients make the connection between features visible rather than assumed.
Testing & reflection

After handing the coursework in, I ran short, informal sessions with a few students on the core "What can I cook?" flow and the pantry tracker, checking whether the ingredient-selection interaction was obvious without explanation and whether the expiry colour-coding made sense without the legend.

I didn't formally record those sessions, so the next step is structured testing: set tasks with clear success criteria, notes on where people hesitate, and a round of design changes based on what comes up.

The CS + UX combination is the throughline here — this is one of the few case studies in this portfolio where the plan is to eventually build the ingredient-to-recipe flow as working code, not just a polished prototype.
